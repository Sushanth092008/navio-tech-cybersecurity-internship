## 1. Introduction

Phishing is a social-engineering technique in which an attacker uses deceptive messages or websites to persuade a person to reveal information, open a malicious attachment, or take an unsafe action. This project demonstrates common warning signs and defensive responses in a controlled, local training page.

## 2. Objectives

- Understand common phishing techniques, including email-based deception and fake website lures.
- Demonstrate a fake but harmless email template.
- Provide an educational landing page for awareness.
- Explain how participant behavior could be measured in an approved study.
- Recommend countermeasures and awareness practices.

## 3. Scope and safety design

The supplied allocation brief suggests a mock email, a Google Form or HTML demonstration page, and a small informed-consent test group. This implementation uses a local HTML page only. It does not send email, impersonate a real service, collect passwords, transmit analytics, or track individual recipients. The displayed counters are in-memory interactions from the current browser session and reset on refresh.

## 4. Methodology

1. Review common warning signs: sender mismatch, urgency, suspicious destinations, and requests for secrets.
2. Display a clearly labelled mock message using the reserved `.invalid` domain.
3. Let the learner open a defensive checklist or choose the report option.
4. Display local demonstration counters and an explanatory debrief.
5. Use the test checklist to verify behavior and accessibility.

## 5. Results and evidence

The page provides a mock email, an awareness checklist, a report-choice interaction, and three local counters. No real participant group has been recruited or tested. Therefore, no click-through rate, credential-submission rate, or user-awareness percentage is claimed. Screenshots and genuine participant feedback should be added only after an approved study is performed.

## 6. Ethical participant-study protocol

Before any study:
- Obtain written instructor/institutional approval and informed consent.
- Explain the simulation purpose, scope, data collected, and withdrawal process.
- Do not collect passwords, one-time codes, personal messages, or unnecessary identifiers.
- Use a small approved group and a pre-agreed data-retention period.
- Debrief participants promptly and provide educational resources.
- Report only aggregated, anonymized findings.

## 7. Preventive measures

- Verify sender addresses and unexpected requests using a known channel.
- Avoid opening unexpected links or attachments.
- Use MFA, preferably phishing-resistant methods where available.
- Keep browsers, operating systems, and endpoint protection updated.
- Enable email authentication and filtering controls.
- Provide a clear report-phishing process and regular awareness training.

## 8. Limitations

The current artifact is a static local demonstration, not an email campaign or phishing infrastructure. It does not measure real-world user behavior, deliver email, collect recipient-level events, or authenticate users. A participant study and screenshots remain outstanding.

## 9. Conclusion

The project demonstrates how phishing-style urgency and account notices can be used as teaching examples, and presents a safe workflow for inspecting and reporting suspicious messages. The design prioritizes consent, privacy, and non-collection of credentials.
